A sentence becomes a point in space

An embedding model reads a piece of text and returns a long list of numbers, here 1,024 of them. Each list is a position in a space where texts that mean similar things sit close together, even when they share few words.

Below, each stripe is one sentence's embedding, one thin bar per number. The first two sentences say nearly the same thing in different words, and their stripes are almost identical. The third is about something else entirely.

To compare two embeddings, Meaning Maker uses cosine similarity: the angle between the two lists, treated as arrows from the origin. Pointing the same way scores 1, and less related texts score lower. With this model even unrelated sentences stay well above zero (0.37 for the cat and the quarks), so the differences that matter are all in the upper part of the scale.

Comparing each moment to the one before

A single sentence is a small, noisy sample of meaning. A short sentence like "There is nothing surprising in this" barely means anything on its own. So rather than embed sentences one at a time, Meaning Maker embeds a window: the current sentence together with the few before it.

Each step, the window slides forward by one sentence. One sentence joins, the oldest one leaves, and the rest stay put. The new window's embedding is compared with the previous window's. If the sentence that joined carried the text somewhere new, the two windows point in different directions and the similarity drops.

Step through the text below, and try different window sizes.

The window size changes what counts as news. With a window of one, every sentence is compared only with the sentence before it, and the line jumps around: almost any two sentences differ. With bigger windows, consecutive windows share most of their text, so similarity runs high, and only a sentence that really changes the subject can pull it down. In the Moby-Dick and Darwin example, the switch from Ishmael's restlessness to the voyage of the Beagle stands out clearly at a window of eight, where a window of one hides it among ordinary sentence-to-sentence variation.

Windows start over at each paragraph. The first sentence of a paragraph is compared with the whole last window of the paragraph before, which is why paragraph openings often register as new.

The path a text takes

If each window is a point, a whole text is a path through the space. The space has 1,024 dimensions, so the picture below flattens it to the two directions along which this text's windows vary the most. Distances get distorted in the squash, but the shape survives: long jumps are changes of subject, tight clusters are stretches that keep circling the same idea.

From numbers to ink

The shading at the top of this page is the last step. Each sentence gets the similarity between its window and the one before. The least similar sentences in the text are drawn in full ink, the most similar fade toward the paper, and everything else falls in between. The first sentence has nothing to compare with, so it is always drawn in full.

The scale is relative to each text, which keeps the result readable whatever the subject. A text that is repetitive throughout will still show its least repetitive sentences in full ink.
